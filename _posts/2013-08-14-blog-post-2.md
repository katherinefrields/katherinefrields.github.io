Ever growing in scientific and political importance, climate modeling serves as a critical tool in society's battle against, and adaptation to, global climate change. To understand the present and the future of this field we must understand the role of physics, stochasticity, and increasingly, machine learning, in how we can predict our climate's potential futures.

Traditional Approaches
------

Traditional climate models rely heavily on physics to model the interaction between Earth's oceans, atmosphere, and human society. The earth is first separated into grid cells, each containing one column of ocean, earth, and atmosphere. The state variables of each cell (ie. humidity, temperature, wind speed) are used as input into the dynamical core, which solves the physical equations governing the motion of fluid in the atmosphere, moving the model forward one time step (Christensen et al., 2024).

Notably, these individual grid cells can often be quite large, around 100 km, and are often on time scales of 30–60 minutes (Christensen et al., 2024). However, these spatial and temporal scales are too coarse to allow for explicit resolution of the physics occurring on a subgrid level, including the moist convection processes that form cumulus and stratocumulus clouds, both of which play a critical role in governing global temperatures (Schneider et al., 2017; Norman et al., 2022). Thus, parametrisations are used in order to simulate the effect of subgrid physics on each individual column, the mean of which is used as the resulting state of the grid cell after one time-step (Christensen et al., 2024; Norman et al., 2022).

However, these parametrisations consistently result in mean and variability bias, leading to significant errors in long-run climate simulations (Christensen et al., 2024; Norman et al., 2022). This can be largely attributed to the scale-invariance of the equations governing fluid dynamics, namely the navier-stokes equations. Put simply, fluid dynamics occurs simultaneously and dynamically on the full spectrum from micro to macro scales, which means dividing the atmosphere into discrete localized columns will always introduce error to the model (Christensen et al. 2020; Norman et al., 2022; Lovejoy et al., 2013). Additionally, these parametrisations are highly computationally expensive, and limit the temporal and spatial scalability of global climate models, further impeding attempts to increase spatio-temporal resolution in hopes of reducing previously mentioned errors (Christensen et al., 2024; Hu et al,. 2024).

To resolve these issues, researchers have turned to the use of physical constraints, machine learning emulation, and stochastic parametrisations.

Hybrid Physics-ML Models
------

One major area of active research is the development of hybrid physics-machine learning parametrisations.
By training machine learning models on the inputs and outputs of our traditional physics parametrisations, researchers aim to sidestep high computational cost. These cheaper parametrisations could then accommodate higher resolution models, allowing for more explicit resolution of small scale dynamics (Hu et al,. 2024). However, there has been substantial difficulty in extending these machine learning parametrisations over longer time-scales, as their lack of physical constraints can hinder the model's ability to generalize to novel data. Furthermore, there often exists a training gap between offline and online error (Lin et al., 2023). Offline error can be defined as the error of the machine learning model on the inputs and outputs of a single time step, and online error can be defined as the error in the overall climate model when these machine learning models are embedded and run in a dynamic feedback loop to obtain long term climate projections (Hu et al., 2024). Research has demonstrated that loss or gains in offline error are not necessarily reflected in the online error, leading to serious difficulties when optimizing these models (Lin et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, small accumulated online errors can lead to significant deviations in long-run simulations as ml-models struggle to account for coupled dynamics of the climate model and embedded ml-model (Lin et al., 2023).

To address these challenges, researchers have worked on integrating physical constraints into these machine learning models, such as matter conservation, value ranges, and various physical laws (Hu et al., 2024; Beucler et al. 2021). These constraints have demonstrated marked improvement in online stability, and provide a strong footing for future advancement in ml parametrisations.

Stochastic Parametrisation
------


Despite advancements in machine learning parametrisations, there persists substantial bias in both mean and variability for long-term climate predictions (Hu et al,. 2024). A promising approach is the incorporation of stochasticity in model parametrisations.

Stochasticity is an inherent component of weather and climate forecasting. Within meteorology, the incorporation of stochasticity is the norm, not the exception. This often comes in the form of ensemble predictions, whereby weather models are run on slightly different initial conditions, and the outcomes are aggregated in order to find the consistent weather trend (Leutbecher et al., 2017). Another approach is the use of stochastic predictions, whereby the model predicts a probability density function for each variable that is sampled from in order to produce the final prediction (Palmer et al,. 2019). Both approaches have demonstrated substantial reduction in mean and variability bias (Berner et al., 2017; Christensen et al., 2017)
In recent years, such approaches have been applied to climate modeling, leading to similar improvements in bias, such as the improvement of variability in El Niño-Southern Oscillation predictions, and the improvement in long-standing mean precipitation (Christensen et al., 2017; Stronmen et al., 2019).
One critical component of such stochastic parametrisations is the incorporation of spatio-temporal correlation. Specifically, when simulating stochasticity, such as when drawing from a probability density function, there should be correlation between neighboring grid cells, as well as between timesteps (Christensen et al., 2024). Potential methods for this include autoregression, that is the inclusion of the previous timestep as model input, as well as the inclusion of state variables from neighboring columns (Plant et al,. 2008).

Stochastic Parametrisation + Machine Learning
------

At the intersection of these evolving approaches is the incorporation of stochasticity in machine learning parametrisations.

One successful approach has been the use of physically-informed Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) for parametrisations (Parthipan et al., 2022). RNN's work by allowing the model to learn short-term and long-term memory, allowing for the training of sophisticated temporal correlations between timesteps. This learned temporal correlation has demonstrated substantial improvement over traditional auto-regressive approaches, and suggests further potential for model improvement through the use and refinement of temporal correlations (Parthipan et al., 2022).

Another successful implementation of temporal correlation has been through the use of multi-scale time-stepping (Chattopadhyay et al., 2022). Under this structure there are two forecast models, each operating on different timescales, in this case, 1 hour and 12 hours. By using the longer term predictions as input to the smaller timestep model, the smaller timestep model can incorporate long-running patterns. Additionally, since the longer forecast model uses a much larger time step, this approach can reduce the error associated with the accumulation of noise over smaller successive steps (Chattopadhyay et al., 2022). More complex, multi-scale time-step architectures have been proposed for similar dynamical systems, with the potential to further improve model performance through temporal correlation (Liu et al., 2021).
Overall, the further integration of stochasticity in machine learning parametrisations promises substantial opportunities to improve model performance. By refining the use of spatio-temporal correlations and leveraging the rapid development of machine learning techniques, we move one step closer to improving the resolution and accuracy of global climate models, and one step closer to understanding and mitigating the development and impacts of global climate change.
