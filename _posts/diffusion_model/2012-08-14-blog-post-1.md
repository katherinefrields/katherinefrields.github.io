Given a set of data $${x_1, x_2, ..., x_N}$$, defined by the underlying data distribution q(x), our goal is
to synthesize new samples from q(x). However, q(x) is unknown and can not be directly computed. Therefore, we instead
train a model to approximate the true distribution q(x) by learning a predicted distribution $$p_{\theta}(x)$$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x) &: \text {True pdf} \\
p_{\theta}(x) &: \text {Predicted pdf, parameterized by }\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Score-based Generative Modeling
======

Score Function
------
Rather than train a model to predict the $$p_{\theta}(x)$$ directly, we instead train a model to predict the log gradient, 
also known as the score $$s_{\theta}(x)$$ [2]. When the gradient is positive, it means we're moving towards a denser region of the distribution, and when the gradient is negative it means we're moving towards a less dense region. Note that the log is taken in order to normalize very small or large values, reducing roundoff error during computation.

$$s_{\theta}(x) = \nabla _x log (p_{\theta}(x)) = \frac{\partial log(p_{\theta}(x))}{\partial x}$$

In order to train a neural network to model the log gradient, we can define $$p_{\theta}(x)$$ in terms of a function $$f_{\theta}(x)$$, where $$Z_{\theta}$$ is a normalizing constant that ensures our pdf integrates to 1.

$$p_{\theta}(x) = \frac{\exp(f_{\theta}(x))}{Z_{\theta}}$$

 Thus we can simplify our score function 

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{\theta}(x) &= \frac{\partial log(p_{\theta}(x))}{\partial x}\\
&= \frac{\partial log(\frac{\exp(f_{\theta}(x))}{Z_{\theta}})}{\partial x}\\
&= \frac{\partial log(\exp(f_{\theta}(x)))}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial log(Z_{\theta})}{\partial x}\\
&= \frac{\partial log(\exp(f_{\theta}(x)))}{\partial x}\\
&= \frac{\partial f_{\theta}(x)}{\partial x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that the gradient of a constant is 0 so we are left with the score function that is equivalent to the gradient of our function $$f_{\theta}(x)$$. 

By computing the l2 norm between the true and predicted score we can obtain the following objective function:

$$E_{x \sim q(x)}[\frac{1}{2} \parallel s_{\theta}(x) - \frac { \partial log (q(x))}{\partial x}\parallel ^ 2 _2]$$

However, though some methods exist to compute $$\nabla _x log (q(x))$$ these approaches are not 
computationally feasible for high-dimensional data

Denoising Score Matching
------
In order to train our score function without access to $$\nabla _x log (q(x))$$, we can use a method known as desnoising score matching. Denoising score matching functions by first adding noise to our data then training the model to remove the noise [1].

We start by defining our noising function 

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x) &= N(\tilde{x}; x, \sigma ^2 I)\\
\tilde{x}&= x + \epsilon, \epsilon ~ N(0, \sigma ^ 2 I )
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us the following conitional density

$$q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x}) = \int q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x)q(x)dx $$

By computing the l2 norm between the true and predicted score for our noised data we can obtain the following objective function:

$$
E_{\tilde{x} \sim q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x)}[\frac{1}{2} \parallel s_{\theta}(\tilde{x}) - \frac { \partial log (q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x) )}{\partial \tilde{x}}\parallel ^ 2 _2]
$$ 

We can simplify the gradient log to obtain

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac { \partial log (q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x) )}{\partial \tilde{x}} &= 
\frac {\partial  log(\frac{\exp { -\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{\tilde{x} - x}{\sigma}) ^2} {\sigma \sqrt{2 \pi}})}{\partial \tilde {x}}\\
&= \frac{\partial log(exp (-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\tilde{x} - x}{\sigma})^2))}{\partial \tilde{x}} + \frac{\partial log(\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2 \pi}})}{\partial \tilde{x}}\\
&= \frac{\partial (-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\tilde{x} - x}{\sigma})^2)}{\partial \tilde{x}}\\
&= \frac{x-\tilde{x}}{\sigma ^ 2}\\
&= \frac{x-(x + \epsilon)}{\sigma ^ 2}\\
&= \frac{-\epsilon}{\sigma ^2}
\end{aligned}$$

Thus, the denoising score matching objective trains the function $$s_{\theta}(\tilde{x})$$ to predict the noise added to x.



Langevin Dynamics
------
Using our original score $$s_{\theta}(x)$$ we will simulate samples from q(x) [1]. To do so, we use Langevin sampling defined by the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x _t &= x _{t-1} + \frac{\delta}{2} \nabla _x log(p_{\theta}(x_{t-1})) + \sqrt{\delta}z_t \\
 &= x _{t-1} + \frac{\delta}{2} s_{\theta}(x_{t-1}) + \sqrt{\delta}z_t \\
\end{aligned}
$$ 

With a fixed step size $$\delta > 0$$, $$z \sim N(0,1)$$, and initial value $$x _0 \sim \pi(x)$$ where $$\pi$$
is a standard normal prior. Conceptually, at each step we are removing the noise predicted by the score function, and adding in
a small amount of new gaussian noise.

As $$\delta \rightarrow 0$$ and $$T \rightarrow \infty$$, $$x _T \sim q (x)$$.

Noise Conditional Score Networks
------
In practice there are issues that arise with the current samples x produced by langevin dynamic sampling [1]:

1. If the data distribution is supported on a low dimensional manifold—as it is often assumed for many real world datasets—the score will be undefined in the ambient space, and score matching will fail to provide a consistent score estimator. 
2. Scores are often innaccurate in low data density regions
3. Scores in low density regions are small and require many many steps to produce an accurate sample

To solve these issues we will train using denoising score matching. By adding noise to our distribution q(x), this method ensures that our data covers all dimensions and low density regions are filled. Additionally, we will train simultaneously at varying noise levels so that when sampling, we can start at large noise levels and then decrease to lower noise levels (similar to simulated annealing), allowing our sampling to maintain both accuracy and efficiency .


To train our function simultaneously for varying noise levels we use a Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN). 
Let $$\{\sigma _i\} ^ L _{i=1}$$ be a positive geometric sequence, $$ \sigma _1 > \sigma _2 > ... > \sigma _L $$


This results in the following denoising score matching objective function

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_{\sigma}(\theta) &= E_{\tilde{x} \sim q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x)}[\frac{1}{2} \parallel s_{\theta}(\tilde{x}, \sigma) - \frac { \partial log (q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x) )}{\partial \tilde{x}}\parallel ^ 2 _2]\\
 &= E_{\tilde{x} \sim q_{\sigma}(\tilde{x} \mid x)}[\frac{1}{2} \parallel s_{\theta}(\tilde{x}, \sigma) + \frac{\tilde{x}- x}{\sigma ^ 2} \parallel ^ 2 _2]
\end{aligned}
$$ 

We then combine all noise levels to obtain the unified objective

$$
\begin{aligned}
L _ {\{ \sigma _i \} ^ L _{i=1}}(\theta) = \frac {1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^L {\lambda (\sigma _i) l _ {\sigma}(\theta)}
\end{aligned}
$$ 

There are many possible choices of $$\lambda$$. In our case we chose $$\lambda = \sigma _i ^2$$

Thus, we use the following sampling algorithm. We start with our prior $$\tilde{x} _0$$, which is drawn from our prior $$\pi$$. We then loop through all our noise levels and use them to define our step $$\alpha _i$$, which decreases as $$\sigma _i \rightarrow \sigma _L$$. This is equivalent to sampling from $$q _ {\sigma _1}$$ to $$q _ {\sigma _2}$$ all the way to $$q _ {\sigma _L}$$, where $$q _ {\sigma _L}$$ is equivalent to q(x).

Denoising DiffusionProbability Model (DDPM)
======

Denoising Diffusion Probability Models operate by using a markov chain $$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$$ to continually add noise to a known data point x, in a process known as forward diffusion. We then training a model to predict the reverse markov chain $$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$, by learning the parameterized function $$p_{\theta}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$, in a process known as reverse diffusion [3].

Forward Diffusion
------
We chose a tractable distribution as our target prior $$\pi$$, such that $$x_T \sim \pi(y)$$. In our case we choose the standard normal distribution, 
 $$ x_T \sim N(0,1) $$. This gives us the resulting markov kernel 

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) =  N(x^t ; x^{t-1}\sqrt{(1- \beta ^ t)}, I \beta ^ t)$$

By sequentially multiplying our initial distribution $$q(x^0)$$  by this Markov kernel, we 
will end up with our target distribution $$\pi (y)$$. In our case, this noising process continues until $$\sqrt{(1-\beta _ t)} $$ is near 0, and 
$$\beta _ t$$ is near 1. The exact value of $$\beta$$ is controlled by a variance schedule, which is typically linear.

In order to efficiently sample $$x_t$$ when performing forward diffusion, rather than having to sequentially noise each datapoint, we leverage the reparameterization trick.

This results in the closed form 

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon, \epsilon \sim N(0,1)$$

We can use this closed form to draw samples from our intermediate distributions directly, rather than having to compute them sequentially.

The full forward trajectory is defined by the joint distribution of our timesteps.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_1, x_2,...,x_t) = q(x_{0...T}) = q(x_0)\prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})
\end{aligned}
$$ 


Reverse Diffusion
------
We want to know $$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$, but the distribution is analytically intractable as it would require integrating over the entire space x. Thus, we instead train $$p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$ to approximate the function.

$$p(x_{t-1}│x_t) = N(x_{t-1}; \mu _{\theta}(x_t, t), \Sigma _ \theta (x_t, t))$$

Where $$\mu _{\theta}(x_t, t)$$ is the parameterized mean of our distribution and $$\Sigma _ \theta (x_t, t)$$ is the variance. 

To initialize $$p_ \theta (x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$, we set  $$ \Sigma _ \theta (x _t, t) = \sigma _t ^ 2 I$$. Though there are many diferent choices of $$\sigma _t ^2, $$ as a rule of thumb

If $$x_0 \sim N(0,1)$$, we set $$ \sigma _t ^ 2 = \beta _t ^ 2 $$   \\
If $$x_0$$ is set to one point, we set $$ \sigma _t ^ 2 = \tilde{\beta} _t ^ 2 = \frac{1- \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\beta _t $$

In our case, we set $$ \sigma _t ^ 2 = \beta _t ^ 2 $$, where $$\beta _t$$ has a fixed schedule.

The full reverse trajectory is defined by the joint distribution of our timesteps

$$ p(x_1, x_2,...,x_t) = p(x_{0...T}) = p(x_0)\prod_{t=1}^T p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$$ 

Training
------
To train our function $$p_ \theta (x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$, we must determine an objective funciton which measures the distance beteween  $$p_ \theta (x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$ and  $$q (x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$. To do so, we use KL Divergence and derive the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO).

> **KL Divergence:**  
>KL Divergence measures the expected value of the difference in the log likelihoods
> where q(x) is the true probability distribution and p(x) is the predicted distribution
>  
> $$D_{KL}(q||p) =\int q(x)log(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}) = E_{x\sim q}[log(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)})]$$
>  

>**Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):**  
> ELBO uses the KL divergence between true and predicted conditional distributions to define the lower bound 
>$$\begin{aligned} 
>D_{KL}(q(z|x)||p(z|x)) &= \int q(z|x)log(\frac{q(z|x)}{p(z|x)}) \\
>&= \int q(z|x)log({q(z|x)}) - \int q(z|x)log({p(z|x)}) \\
>&= \int q(z|x)log({q(z|x)}) - \int q(z|x)log(\frac{p(z,x)}{p(x)}) \\
>&= \int q(z|x)log({q(z|x)}) - \int q(z|x)log({p(z,x)}) + \int q(z|x)log({p(x)}) \\
>&=  E_{z\sim q}[log({q(z|x)})] - E_{z\sim q}[log({p(z,x)})] + log({p(x)})\int q(z|x) \\
>&=  E_{z\sim q}[log({q(z|x)})] - E_{z\sim q}[log({p(z,x)})] + log({p(x)}) \\
> log({p(x)}) &= D_{KL}(q(z|x)||p(z|x)) -  E_{z\sim q}[log({q(z|x)})] + E_{z\sim q}[log({p(z,x)})] \\
>\end{aligned}$$  
>  
> Since KL divergence is always >0
> 
> $$log({p(x)}) \geq -  \underbrace{E_{z\sim q}[log({q(z|x)})] + E_{z\sim q}[log({p(z,x)})]}_{\text{ELBO}}$$
> 
> Since the log likelihood of p will remain constant for a given x, by minimizing the 
> right hand terms you indirectly minimize the KL Divergence.


Thus, we can train our model to minimize the KL Divergence  $$ D_{KL}(q(x_{1...T}|x_0)||p(x_{1...T}|x_0))$$.
Thereby minimizing the distance between the true and predicted reverse trajectories for a given image $$x_0$$.
This is also equivalent to maximizing the expected value of $$p(x_0)$$ given $$x_0 \sim q$$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
 E_{x\sim q}[log({p(x_0)})] &\geq -  E_{x\sim q}[log({q(x_{1...T}|x_0)})] + E_{x\sim q}[log({p(x_0,x_{1...T})})]\\   
 E_{x\sim q}[-log({p(x_0)}]) &\leq  E_{x\sim q}[log({q(x_{1...T}|x_0)})] - E_{x\sim q}[log({p(x_0,x_{1...T})})]\\   
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(\frac{p(x_0,x_{1...T})}{q(x_{1...T}|x_0)})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(\frac{p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_{t-1}|x_t)} {\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x_{t}|x_{t-1})})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=1}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_t|x_{t-1})})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=1}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_t|x_{t-1})}) - log(\frac{p(x_0|x_1)}{q(x_1|x_0)})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)})(\frac{q(x_{t-1}|x_0)}{q(x_t|x_0)}) - log(\frac{p(x_0|x_1)}{q(x_1|x_0)})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)}) - \sum_{t=2}^{T}(\frac{q(x_{t-1}|x_0)}{q(x_t|x_0)}) - log(\frac{p(x_0|x_1)}{q(x_1|x_0)})]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)}) - \sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{q(x_{t-1}|x_0)}{q(x_t|x_0)}) - log(p(x_0|x_1)) + log(q(x_1|x_0))]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)}) - log(\frac{q(x_1|x_0)}{q(x_T|x_0)}) + log(q(x_1|x_0)) - log(p(x_0|x_1)) ]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[-log(p(x_T)) -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)}) - log(\frac{1}{q(x_T|x_0)}) - log(p(x_0|x_1)) ]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{-log(p(x_T))}{q(x_T|x_0)} -\sum_{t=2}^{T}log(\frac{p(x_{t-1}|x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)}) - log(p(x_0|x_1)) ]\\
&= E_{x\sim q}{\underbrace{D_{KL}(q(x_T|x_0)||p(x_T))}_{L_T} + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{D_{KL}(q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)||p(x_{t-1}|x_t))}_{L_{T-1}} - \underbrace{log(p(x_0|x_1))}_{L_0}}
\end{aligned}
$$

This simplified expression seperates out our terms into $$L_T$$, $$L_{T-1}$$, and $$L_0$$. Our $$L_T$$ term is made up of our forward diffusion $$q(x_T \mid x_0)$$ and our prior distribution $$p(x_T)$$. Since both of these are known, we can ignore $$L_T$$ when training.

Thus what remains is training a model to minimize $$L_{T-1}$$. Since $$L_{T-1}$$ is the the KL divergence between $$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \mid \mid p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$ and we have fixed the variance of each to be $$\beta _t ^2$$, minimizing the KL divergence is equivalent to minimzing the distance between their means.

Thus, we aim to define the mean of $$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$$.

> & \propto exp(-\frac{(x- \mu) ^2}{2\sigma ^2})\\
> &= exp(-\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x^2 -2 \mu x + \mu ^ 2)}{\sigma ^2})
>\end{aligned}$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0) &= q(x_t | x_{t-1}, x_0)\frac{q(x_t-1 | x_0)}{q(x_t | x_0)}\\
&= \frac{N(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}}x_0, (1-\alpha _t)I) - N(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t-1)I})}{N(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I)}\\

&= \exp [-\frac{1}{2}[(\frac{\alpha _t}{\beta _t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}) x^2 _{t-1} - (\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha _t} x_t}{\beta _t} + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})x_{t-1} + \frac{x^2}{\beta _t} + \frac{\bar {\alpha}_{t-1} x^2 _0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} - \frac{(x_t-1 - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0)^2}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} ]]\\

&= \exp [-\frac{1}{2}[(\frac{\alpha _t}{\beta _t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}) x^2 _{t-1} - (\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha _t} x_t}{\beta _t} + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})x_{t-1} + C(x_t, x_0) ]]\\

&\propto \exp [-\frac{1}{2}[(\frac{\alpha _t}{\beta _t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}) x^2 _{t-1} - (\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha _t} x_t}{\beta _t} + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})x_{t-1}]]
\end{aligned}
$$


Since our collection of terms $$C(x_t, x_0)$$ does not contain $$x_{t-1}$$, we can treat it as a constant and ignore it. 

Now, to rewrite this as a normal distribution, we must identify the mean and variance of our distribution. According to the Normal PDF described earlier, we expect our $$x^2$$ term to have a coefficient of 1 and our x term to have a coefficient of $$-2\mu$$. To satifsy this constrain, we can define our variance as:

$$\tilde{\beta}_t = (\frac{\alpha _t}{\beta _t} + \frac {1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})^{-1}$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0) &= \exp [-\frac{1}{2}[ \frac{x^2 _{t-1}}{\tilde{\beta} _t} - (\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha _t} x_t}{\beta _t} + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})x_{t-1}]]\\
&= \exp [-\frac{1}{2}[\frac{x^2 _{t-1} - \tilde{\beta} _t(\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha _t} x_t}{\beta _t} + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})x_{t-1}}{\tilde{\beta} _t}]]
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now define our $$\mu$$ as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mu}(x_t, x_0) &= \tilde{\beta} _t (\frac{x_t \sqrt{\alpha _t}}{\beta _t} + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}})\\
&= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha}_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})x_t + \beta _t\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}x_0}}{\alpha _t - \alpha _t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} + \beta _t}\\
&= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha _t} (1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 -\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t + \frac{\beta _t \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the parameterization $$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon$$, we can substitue for $$x_0$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mu} &= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha _t} (1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 -\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t + \frac{\beta _t \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon _t}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\\
&= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha _t} (1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 -\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t + \frac{\beta _t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon _t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\\
&= \frac{(\alpha _t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + \beta _t)x_t - \beta _t \sqrt{1- \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon _t}{\sqrt{\alpha _t}(1- \bar{\alpha _t})}\\
&= \frac{(1- \bar{\alpha}_t) x_t - \beta _t \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon _t}{\sqrt{\alpha _t}(1-\bar{\alpha} _t)}\\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha _t}}(x_t - \frac{\beta _t}{\sqrt{1- \bar{\alpha} _t}}) \epsilon _t
\end{aligned}
$$


Thus, we can redefine $$L_{t-1}$$ in terms of the difference in the means of $$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$$ and $$p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{t-1} &=  E_{x\sim q}[D_{KL}(q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)||p(x_{t-1}|x_t))]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{1}{2 \sigma _t ^2} \parallel \tilde{\mu}_t (x_t, x_0) - \mu_{\theta} (x_t, x_t) \parallel ^2 ]\\
 &=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{1}{2 \sigma _t ^2} \parallel \tilde{\mu}_t (x_t, x_0) - \mu_{\theta} (x_t, t) \parallel ^2 ]\\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{1}{2 \sigma _t ^2} \parallel \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}(x_t - \frac{\beta _t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon) - \mu_{\theta} (x_t, t) \parallel ^2] \\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{1}{2 \sigma _t ^2} \parallel \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}(x_t - \frac{\beta _t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha _t}}(x_t - \frac{\beta _t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon _{\theta} (x_t, t)) \parallel ^2] \\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[\frac{1}{2 \sigma _t ^2}\parallel  \frac{-\beta _t}{\sqrt{\alpha _t} \sqrt{1- \bar{\alpha} _t}}(\epsilon _t - \epsilon _{\theta}(x_t, t)) \parallel ^2] \\
&=  E_{x\sim q}[ \frac{\beta ^2 _t}{2 \sigma _t ^2 (\alpha _t) (1- \bar{\alpha} _t)} \parallel (\epsilon _t - \epsilon _{\theta}(x_t, t)) \parallel ^2] \\
\end{aligned}
$$
