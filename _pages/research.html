AI Generation of Thermodynamic Histories From Ice Crystal Images

Columbia University, LEAP

Giorgia Nicolaou, Katherine Frields, Troy Stephens, Kara Lamb, Joseph Ko, Kara Sulia, Jade Cai

The diversity of ice crystal morphology is a key property in the radiative forcing of cirrus clouds. Though it is understood that this morphology is a result of thermodynamic history, traditional first-principles crystal growth models as well as bulk parameterizations based on instantaneous conditions fail to capture crystal diversity. In this work, we developed a conditional diffusion model capable of generating realistic thermodynamic histories for cirrus cloud ice crystals based solely on final crystal morphology. The results provide a firm basis for improving our understanding of ice crystal growth and the development of new bulk-parametrizations in cirrus cloud models.

No-Compromise Uncertainty Quantification in a Stable Machine Learning Emulator of Subgrid Climate Physics

University of California, Irvine

Katherine Frields, Jerry Lin, Mike Pritchard

The diffculties in modeling cloud formation has contributed significantly to the uncertainty in long-term climate projections. These challenges are rooted in the high compute power necessary to run climate models at a high enough resolution to capture the physics associated with the formation of these clouds. To circumvent this issue, researchers have attempted to replace conventional cloud resolving models with machine learning models that can run at a fraction of the compute power. However, the development of stable and high fidelity models has proven diffcult, as small mistakes in the model can compound over the course of long-term climate simulations leading to significant deviations from their conventional cloud resolving model counterparts. Some recent innovations that have improved machine learning model performance include the introduction of physics-based constraints on the model, as well as a model architecture known as a U-net Convolutional Neural Network. In this work, we introduce uncertainty quantification to our model by training it to predict the uncertainty associated with its outputs. This has provided insight into areas of uncertainty in our model, without resulting in any apparent loss in model performance.

Comparative Analysis of Parsimoniuous vs. Established Evapotranspiration Models

University of California, Irvine

Katherine Frields, Angela Rigden

Evaporative fraction serves as a critical indicator of water stress and is used in agricultural planning and climate modeling as well drought evaluation. The current evapotranspiration models from which these evaporative fractions are derived often taken a high number of complex variables such as soil mositure, land cover type, and plant physiology. This complexity results in models that can be difficult to constrain and struggle to achieve a high accuracy. Furthermore, evaporatranspiration observations are difficult to collect, and thus the recent and historical record is limited. However, new research has suggested a simpler approach may be possible, by relying on the natural embedding of land-surface data into atmopheric observations. Under this hypothesis, and assuming a state of "surface flux equilibrium", it may be possible that evaporative fractions could be estimated using only land surface temperature and specifc humidity. This simple, parsimonious model would enable the evaluation and constraining of existing models, as well as the production of historical datasets and remotely sensed evaporative fraction. In this work, I implemented a simple, parsimoniuous model using the GridMET dataset, and evaluated its performance against existing evapotranspiration models obtained via OpenET.
